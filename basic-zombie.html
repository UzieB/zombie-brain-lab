Basic Zombie

Field note

Field note. Subject advances at a slow, even pace and stops only to eat whatever plant is in its path. It makes no attempt to avoid incoming fire. After losing its head, it keeps walking for a moment before falling.

Region guide
Prefrontal cortex
The front of the frontal lobe. Supports planning and higher-level thinking.
Amygdala
A small, almond-shaped area deep in the temporal lobe. Processes emotion, especially fear and aggression.
Cerebellum
At the back, below the cortex. Controls balance and coordination; helps with skills and habits.
Brainstem
Connects the brain to the spinal cord. Runs automatic functions such as breathing and heart rate.
Spinal cord
Not part of the brain. Carries messages between the brain and body and runs some reflexes on its own.

Sources: Spielman et al. (2020), §3.4, §8.2, §10.4. (Unchecked by a person; AI research only.)

Brain report

(open it after you make your guess)

What’s different. This zombie’s prefrontal cortex and amygdala are smaller than the control brain’s and look damaged. Its cerebellum and brainstem look typical, and its spinal cord is unusually thick.

Why it might not plan. The prefrontal cortex supports higher-level thinking such as planning (Spielman et al., 2020, §3.4). (Unchecked by a person; AI research only.) With less of it, the zombie might head in a straight line instead of looking for a better route.

Why it might not dodge. The amygdala helps process emotion, especially fear (Spielman et al., 2020, §8.2). (Unchecked by a person; AI research only.) A smaller amygdala could mean a weaker response to danger, which might explain why the zombie keeps coming under fire.

How it still walks. Walking steadily takes balance and coordination, and the cerebellum handles those (Spielman et al., 2020, §3.4). (Unchecked by a person; AI research only.) This zombie’s cerebellum looks typical.

How it might walk without a head. In people, the spinal cord handles some reflexes without the brain, such as pulling your hand away from something hot (Spielman et al., 2020, §3.4). (Unchecked by a person; AI research only.) Animal research suggests the spinal cord also contains circuits that can produce a basic stepping rhythm (Guertin, 2009). (Unchecked by a person; AI research only.) This zombie’s extra-thick spinal cord might let it keep stepping for a moment after losing its brain. A human couldn’t do this.

Real-brain takeaway

  • The prefrontal cortex supports planning and higher-level thinking.
  • The amygdala processes emotion, especially fear.
  • The cerebellum handles balance and coordination.
  • The spinal cord carries messages between the brain and body and runs some reflexes on its own.
